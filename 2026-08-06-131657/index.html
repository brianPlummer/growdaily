What the knowledge base says

Inspect the trichomes with a loupe or a USB scope at 60–100x. Look at the buds and the calyxes. Do not look at the sugar leaf tips. Below ~30x you cannot tell clear from cloudy. Then read the colors:

Why the earlier estimates were wrong

The 7/19 report gave "~1 week to harvest." That week ended on 7/26. The plant is now 11 days past that date and it is still not ripe. The reason is mechanical, not a small error:

  • Pistil color measures the age of each pistil. It does not measure resin maturity.
  • This plant foxtails. A foxtail spire grows new bracts and new white pistils on top of an old, ripe bud. The plant therefore keeps producing young pistils.
  • A canopy-level photo mixes old ripe pistils with new pistils. The eye reads the ripe ones and over-scores ripeness.

Rule for the rest of this grow: the loupe decides, not the pistils and not the calendar.

2 · Foxtail spires grew larger Worse — measurement needed

The 8/1 report opened this item at 2 top colas. Five days later the spires are taller and I can see them in more places: the top-left cola, the top-right cola, all 3 cola macros, and at canopy level in photo 1.

The knowledge base splits foxtailing into two causes. Stress foxtail appears only at the tops nearest the light. It looks irregular. The new growth is pale or bleached. It travels with other heat or light stress signs, such as tacoing and bleaching. The triggers are a canopy temperature above ~85–86 °F, or light that is too close or too strong. Genetic foxtail appears evenly across many bud sites. The plant shows no other stress sign. It is benign and it does not reduce potency or yield.

How this case scores now. The score moved slightly toward the benign side since 8/1:

  • For stress foxtail: the spires appear at the tops nearest the light, and the new growth is pale.
  • For genetic foxtail: no leaf tacos. No leaf bleaches. The spire calyxes read pale green, not white — a bleached spire reads white or near-white, and these do not. The spires now appear at many sites, not only two, and that moves the pattern toward "even across many bud sites."
  • Third possible cause: a late pistil flush. This is common when a plant runs past its finish date. This plant is 8 days past it.

I still cannot decide between the three, and the reason is the same as on 8/1. One measurement decides it. Measure the canopy temperature. No temperature reading exists anywhere in grow_context.md for the whole grow. This is the second report to ask. If the canopy runs below ~85 °F, stress foxtail is unlikely and you can ignore this item.

Consequence if it is stress foxtail: low, at this point. You cannot reverse a spire that already formed. You can only stop new ones, and the plant has 5 to 12 days left. Potency does not drop. The practical value of the measurement is for the next grow.

Also measure the PPFD at the two top colas. The last reading was just below 800, taken on 7/13.

3 · Interior leaf yellowing — I reopen this item Reopened

What changed. The 8/1 report closed this item. It called the yellowing plain senescence and removed it from the watch list. That decision used four age tests from the senescence chapter, and the plant passed all four. But the 8/1 set contained no single-leaf macro photo. The closure therefore rested on canopy-level frames only. Your four leaf macros are new evidence, and they do not support the closure. I reopen the item.

The pattern mismatch. The knowledge base states that natural senescence produces uniform whole-leaf yellowing, not just between the veins. Your four leaves do not show that. They show:

  • Yellow that starts at the leaflet tip.
  • Yellow that traces down the serrated margin, tooth by tooth.
  • Green tissue that stays in the leaf centre and along the veins.
  • Small brown necrotic patches inside the yellow zone on 3 of the 4 leaves.

What that pattern matches. The deficiency chapter describes the potassium early tell in these words: before the edges burn, the yellowing traces the leaf serrations and makes a thin yellow outline around the margin. The later stage is burnt or yellow leaf edges and tips on older leaves, sometimes with spots. Your photos match both stages. The 7/13 report saw the same pattern on 3 leaves and called it the potassium early tell. That read was correct and 8/1 dropped it.

What I do not claim. I do not diagnose a potassium deficiency. Three points hold me back:

  • Senescence itself remobilizes potassium out of old leaves and into the buds. A late-flower plant therefore produces potassium-pattern symptoms as part of a normal finish.
  • The knowledge base warns that potassium lockout from pH drift presents identically. You must verify pH before you add potassium. This grow has no pH data.
  • The four age tests still pass: late in the cycle, strictly bottom-up, slow over weeks, and trichomes now confirmed as going cloudy. The trichome tiebreaker points at senescence.

My read. A late-flower potassium draw-down sits on top of normal senescence. Confidence: moderate. This is not one or the other. The pattern is real and the age context is also real.

The action does not change. Do not feed potassium now. Do not flush. This is living soil in a no-till bed, and the plant is 5 to 12 days from the cut. The knowledge base is explicit: a living-soil plant senesces on its own, there is no leachable salt reservoir to flush, and the correct finish is to let it fade on water. A correction applied now cannot reach the buds in time, and it adds risk.

The value of this flag is for the next grow. Raise potassium earlier. Put more potassium in the flowering top dress, or add a potassium-rich amendment in week 4 to 5 of flower instead of week 6 and later. Also start recording pH.
